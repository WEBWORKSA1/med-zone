Clinical trials are how new treatments become standard care. Joining one can give access to a promising therapy, close monitoring and the satisfaction of helping future patients. It also involves time, uncertainty and some risk, so it is worth understanding before you sign up.

## What a clinical trial is

A clinical trial tests a medical intervention in people: a drug, device, procedure, diet or behaviour change. Trials run in phases, from small safety studies (phase 1) to large comparisons with current treatment (phase 3) and long-term monitoring after approval (phase 4). Many trials also need healthy volunteers.

## How to search

1. **Start with your condition** in our [trial finder](../../trials/), which searches ClinicalTrials.gov live. Filter by "recruiting" and your location.
2. **Read the eligibility criteria.** Age, diagnosis, previous treatments and other health conditions all matter.
3. **Contact the study team** listed on the record, or ask your specialist. Many hospitals also run their own trial registries.
4. **Consider a matching service.** Recruitment partners can screen several studies at once; use our [free matching form](../../connect/?intent=trials) and we'll pass your request on.

## Questions to ask the study team

- What is the purpose of the study, and what is being compared?
- Could I receive a placebo? Can I keep taking my current treatment?
- What tests, visits and time are involved, and for how long?
- What are the known risks and possible benefits?
- Will travel, parking or lost time be paid for? Who pays if I'm harmed?
- Will I learn the results? What happens to my care after the study ends?

## Your rights

Before you join, you'll go through **informed consent**: a discussion and document that explain the study in plain language. You can take it home, discuss it with family and your own doctor, and ask anything. Trials are reviewed by an ethics committee (an institutional review board in the U.S.). Most importantly, you can **leave a trial at any time**, for any reason, without losing your usual care.

## Is a trial right for you?

Trials may suit you if standard treatments haven't worked, if you want access to newer approaches, or if contributing to research matters to you. They may not suit you if you can't manage the visit schedule or if a proven treatment is available that you'd rather start now. Talk it through with your doctor.
