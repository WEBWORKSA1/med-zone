A lab report can look like a wall of numbers, abbreviations and red flags. Most of it becomes readable once you know how the report is built and what "normal" actually means.

## The parts of a lab report

Each line usually shows four things:

1. **The test name**, often abbreviated (Na for sodium, ALT for alanine aminotransferase).
2. **Your result** with its unit, such as mmol/L or mg/dL.
3. **The reference range** the laboratory uses for people like you.
4. **A flag**, typically H (high) or L (low), when your result is outside that range.

Our [lab values sheet](../../learn/lab-values/) lists typical adult ranges in both US and SI units, but the range printed on your own report is the one that applies, because it reflects that lab's equipment and population.

## What "normal" really means

Reference ranges are usually built so that 95 percent of healthy people fall inside them. That has a surprising consequence: about 1 in 20 healthy people will be flagged on any single test, and if you have 20 tests in a panel, at least one flag is likely even when nothing is wrong.

So a single value that sits just outside the range is common and often harmless. Clinicians look at:

- **How far outside** the range the result is.
- **The trend**: a creatinine that has crept up over three years means more than a single borderline value.
- **The pattern**: several related results moving together (for example ALT, AST and bilirubin) tell a clearer story than one.
- **Your situation**: symptoms, medicines, pregnancy, recent exercise and hydration all shift results.

## Common panels in plain language

**Complete blood count (CBC).** Hemoglobin and hematocrit show whether you have enough red blood cells (low means anemia). White cells rise with infection or inflammation. Platelets help blood clot.

**Basic or comprehensive metabolic panel (BMP, CMP).** Sodium, potassium, chloride and bicarbonate are electrolytes. Creatinine and urea reflect kidney function, and the lab converts creatinine into an [eGFR](../../tools/egfr-calculator/). The comprehensive panel adds liver tests (ALT, AST, ALP, bilirubin) and proteins such as albumin.

**Lipid panel.** Total, LDL and HDL cholesterol plus triglycerides. LDL is the main target for lowering heart risk.

**HbA1c.** Average blood sugar over two to three months; use our [A1C converter](../../tools/a1c-calculator/) to translate it into everyday glucose numbers.

**TSH.** The pituitary's signal to the thyroid. A high TSH usually means the thyroid is underactive.

## Results that deserve a prompt call

Labs phone clinicians about "critical values" such as very high or low potassium, sodium or glucose, because these can be dangerous within hours. If you see a result far outside the range and haven't heard from your care team, call them rather than waiting for the next appointment. If you feel unwell, with chest pain, confusion, severe weakness or fainting, seek urgent care.

## Questions to ask about your results

- Which results are outside the range, and do they matter for me?
- Is this a change from my previous tests?
- Could a medicine, supplement or recent illness explain it?
- Do I need a repeat test, and when?
- What can I do to improve this number?

## Getting your results

In many countries patients now have the right to see results in an online portal, sometimes before their clinician has reviewed them. That speed is useful but can be stressful. It is fine to wait for your clinician's interpretation, and it is also fine to send a message asking what a result means.
