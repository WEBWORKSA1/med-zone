Most medication errors don't happen in hospitals; they happen at home, with the wrong dose, a doubled-up tablet or a risky combination. A few habits remove most of the danger.

## Keep one current medicine list

Write down every prescription, over-the-counter medicine, vitamin and supplement you take, with the dose, the time of day and the reason. Update it whenever anything changes and bring it to every appointment and pharmacy visit. Keep a photo on your phone and a copy in your wallet for emergencies.

## Use one pharmacy if you can

A single pharmacy sees your whole list and its software flags interactions and duplicates, such as two products that both contain acetaminophen (paracetamol).

## Read the label, every time

Check the name, strength and directions when you pick up a refill. Generic tablets can change shape and color between fills; if a tablet looks different, ask the pharmacist before taking it. You can read the full official label for any U.S. medicine with our [drug lookup](../../drugs/).

## Watch for hidden doubles

- **Acetaminophen** appears in many cold, flu and sleep products as well as in prescription painkillers. Too much can damage the liver.
- **NSAIDs** (ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin) appear in many combination products and can harm the stomach and kidneys, especially in older adults.
- **Sedating antihistamines** (such as diphenhydramine) appear in sleep aids and allergy and cold medicines.

## Measure liquids properly

Use the oral syringe or dosing cup that comes with the medicine, never a kitchen spoon, and check whether the dose is in mL. For children, doses depend on weight in kilograms; mixing up pounds and kilograms is a common and serious error.

## Build a routine

Tie doses to daily habits, such as brushing your teeth. Use a weekly pill organizer and phone reminders. If you miss a dose, check the leaflet or ask a pharmacist; doubling up is not always safe.

## Store medicines safely

Keep medicines in their original containers, away from heat and humidity (a bathroom cabinet is often the worst place), and up and out of sight of children. Lock up opioids, sedatives and stimulants.

## Dispose of leftovers

Use a drug take-back site or pharmacy kiosk where available. If there is none, the FDA's guidance explains which medicines can be mixed with coffee grounds or cat litter and thrown away, and which few should be flushed.

## Know when to ask

Call your pharmacist or prescriber before stopping a medicine because of side effects, before adding a new supplement, and whenever you're unsure. For a suspected overdose or poisoning, contact your local poison centre (in the U.S., 1-800-222-1222) or emergency services.
