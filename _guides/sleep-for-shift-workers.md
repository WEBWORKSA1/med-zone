Nurses, paramedics, factory workers, drivers and security staff keep the world running at night, often at the cost of their sleep. Your body clock is built to sleep at night, so daytime sleep is shorter and lighter. You can't fully beat biology, but you can tilt the odds.

## Protect a consistent sleep block

Pick a main sleep window and defend it on work days, such as 8:30 am to 3:30 pm after a night shift. Tell family and friends, silence your phone and use a "do not disturb" sign. Use our [sleep cycle calculator](../../tools/sleep-cycle-calculator/) to time your alarm for the end of a cycle.

## Control light

Light is the strongest signal to your body clock.

- **During the shift:** bright light, especially in the first half, helps alertness.
- **On the way home:** wear sunglasses to reduce morning light.
- **At home:** blackout curtains or an eye mask; keep screens dim before bed.

## Make the bedroom a cave

Cool (around 16 to 19 °C or 60 to 67 °F), dark and quiet. Earplugs or a white-noise machine help with daytime traffic and household noise.

## Use naps strategically

- A **20 to 30 minute nap before a night shift** improves alertness.
- A **short break nap during the shift**, if your workplace allows, can reduce errors. Keep it short to avoid grogginess, or allow a full 90-minute cycle.

## Time caffeine

Caffeine early in the shift helps; stop about 6 hours before your planned sleep. Caffeine has a half-life of around 5 hours, so a 4 am coffee can still be active at 10 am.

## Eat and drink with your clock in mind

Large meals in the middle of the night sit poorly. Favour a main meal before the shift and lighter snacks overnight, and stay hydrated, then taper fluids before sleep so you're not woken up.

## The drive home is the danger zone

Drowsy driving after night shifts is a major risk. If you're struggling to keep your eyes open, nap before driving, use public transit, or get a ride.

## Days off and rotating shifts

On days off, a compromise schedule (going to bed later and waking later than on a day schedule) eases the switch. Forward-rotating schedules (days, then evenings, then nights) are easier on the body clock than backward rotation.

## When to get help

Persistent insomnia, falling asleep at work despite good sleep habits, or loud snoring with breathing pauses deserve medical attention. Shift work disorder and sleep apnea are treatable.
