Health information has never been easier to find, or harder to judge. Use these questions on any article, video, post or AI answer before it shapes a decision.

## 1. Who is behind it?

Look for an "About" page, named authors and an editorial policy. Government health agencies, academic medical centres and professional societies have reputations to protect. Be cautious when the source sells the product it praises.

## 2. What is the evidence?

Good health content says where its claims come from and links to them. Check the type of evidence:

- **Systematic reviews and meta-analyses** combine many studies and carry the most weight.
- **Randomized controlled trials** test cause and effect.
- **Observational studies** show associations, not proof: people who drink coffee may differ in many ways from those who don't.
- **Animal and lab studies** are early clues that often don't hold up in people.
- **Anecdotes and testimonials** are stories, not evidence.

You can look up studies yourself with our [research finder](../../research/).

## 3. How big is the effect?

"Cuts risk by 50 percent" sounds large. If risk falls from 2 in 1,000 to 1 in 1,000, the absolute change is 0.1 percentage points. Look for absolute numbers and the number of people studied.

## 4. Is it current?

Guidelines change. Check the date and whether the page has been reviewed recently, especially for fast-moving topics like vaccines, new drugs and screening.

## 5. Does it sound too good to be true?

Red flags include "miracle" or "secret" cures, one product for many unrelated diseases, pressure to buy now, attacks on "mainstream medicine", and advice to stop prescribed treatment.

## 6. Is it about people like you?

A study in young men may not apply to older women. A treatment tested for one condition may not work for another.

## Using AI answers and calculators wisely

AI tools and online calculators, including ours, are useful for understanding and preparing questions. Treat them as a starting point: check important claims against the cited sources, and discuss decisions with someone who knows your history.
