High blood pressure rarely causes symptoms, which is why it is called the silent killer. It is also one of the most treatable risks for stroke, heart attack, heart failure and kidney disease. Knowing your numbers is the first step.

## Two numbers, two moments

A reading such as 128/82 mmHg has two parts:

- **Systolic (top):** the pressure in your arteries when the heart squeezes.
- **Diastolic (bottom):** the pressure when the heart relaxes between beats.

Both matter. Systolic pressure becomes the stronger predictor of risk after about age 50, as arteries stiffen.

## Categories for adults

| Category | Systolic | | Diastolic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | below 120 | and | below 80 |
| Elevated | 120 to 129 | and | below 80 |
| Stage 1 hypertension | 130 to 139 | or | 80 to 89 |
| Stage 2 hypertension | 140 or higher | or | 90 or higher |

These are the 2017 American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association categories. Some countries still define hypertension from 140/90, but the direction of advice is the same: lower is better, down to about 120 systolic for most adults.

A reading above 180/120 needs prompt attention. With chest pain, shortness of breath, back pain, numbness, weakness, vision change or trouble speaking, call emergency services.

## Measuring accurately at home

Home readings often reveal "white-coat" hypertension (high only at the clinic) or the opposite, "masked" hypertension. To get reliable numbers:

1. Use a validated upper-arm cuff that fits: the bladder should wrap most of the way around your arm.
2. Avoid caffeine, smoking and exercise for 30 minutes before.
3. Empty your bladder, sit with your back supported and feet flat, and rest for 5 minutes.
4. Rest your arm on a table so the cuff is at heart level. Don't talk.
5. Take two readings a minute apart, morning and evening, for a week, and share the average with your clinician.

## What lowers blood pressure

Lifestyle changes can each lower systolic pressure by several points, and they add up:

- **Less sodium:** most comes from packaged and restaurant food, not the salt shaker.
- **More potassium:** vegetables, fruit, beans and dairy, unless you've been told to limit potassium.
- **The DASH eating pattern**, rich in vegetables, fruit, whole grains and low-fat dairy.
- **Regular activity:** 150 minutes of moderate exercise a week.
- **Weight loss** if you carry extra weight.
- **Less alcohol**, and no smoking.
- **Enough sleep**, and treatment for sleep apnea if you snore and feel tired.

When medicines are needed, several well-tested classes exist, and many people need two at low doses. Taking them every day matters more than which one you start with.

## Questions for your next visit

- What is my target blood pressure?
- Should I measure at home, and how often?
- Could any of my medicines or supplements be raising it? (Decongestants and NSAID painkillers can.)
- What is my overall heart risk, not just my blood pressure?
